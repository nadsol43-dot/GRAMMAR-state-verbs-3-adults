STATE VERBS

also called stative verbs — they describe states, not actions

💡 State verbs say what something is, has, senses, thinks or feels. You can't perform them as an activity — that's why they normally stay in simple tenses, not –ing.
1 Being & perception — what is, has & senses

Being & descriptions

what something is / seems like
be exist seem appear look sound resemble

She is tired. He seems friendly. It looks nice.

Possession

what someone has
have possess own belong

I have a phone. She owns a small shop.

Senses

what we perceive
see hear smell taste feel touch

👁 eyes → see · 👂 ears → hear · 👃 nose → smell · 👅 tongue → taste

2 Mind & feelings — what we think, want & love

Thinking & ideas

opinions & mental states
know think imagine believe doubt suppose recognize remember forget understand mean guess realize agree matter

I know the answer. It doesn't matter.

Emotions

what we love / hate / enjoy
love like adore care for hate dislike mind envy amaze astonish

I love music. Do you mind?

Attitudes & desires

wants, hopes & values
want need wish hope desire prefer appreciate value

I want coffee. She prefers tea.

3 Body & the world — physical feelings, quantities & links

Physical sensations

what the body feels
hurt ache itch

My head hurts. My tooth aches. My nose itches.

Measurements & value

weight, size & cost
measure weigh cost consist of contain

It costs $5. The bag weighs 2 kg.

Relationships

how things are connected
belong to depend on owe

This book belongs to me. It depends on you.

⚠ The golden rule

State verbs are normally NOT used in continuous (–ing) tenses:

❌ I am knowing the answer. ✅ I know the answer.
❌ I am wanting a new phone. ✅ I want a new phone.

🔁 But meaning can change!

  • think = believe → thinking = considering
  • have = possess → having lunch = eating
  • see = perceive → seeing = meeting
  • feel = sense or opinion → feeling = checking by touch
  • taste = flavour → tasting = trying
  • smell = have a scent → smelling = sniffing
  • touch = be in contact → touching = actively making contact
  • look = seem → looking = searching / watching
  • appear = seem → appearing = performing
  • fit = be the right size → fitting = putting together
4 In focus — both forms work: feel · look · ache & hurt — then touch: meaning decides

Feel — stative or dynamic?

health & emotions: both forms are fine — opinions and checks by touch follow their own rules

✓ Both forms work — same meaning

SIMPLE"I feel good."
— a general, stable state of well-being

CONTINUOUS"I am feeling good."
— how I feel right now, this moment

"How do you feel?" ≈ "How are you feeling?" — native speakers use both.

⚠ The meaning changes the rules

OPINION · SIMPLE ONLY"I feel that this is a mistake." ✗ "I am feeling that..."

BY TOUCH · –ING OK"The doctor is feeling my pulse." — an action

The form depends on WHICH meaning of feel you use.

Look — stative or dynamic?

appearance: both forms are fine — but "look at" and "look for" are different, active verbs

✓ Both forms work — same meaning

SIMPLE"You look good."
— a general fact, timeless

CONTINUOUS"You are looking good."
— right now: new style, today's outfit

"How do I look?" ≈ "How am I looking?" — absolutely interchangeable.

⚠ The meaning changes → they become actions

DIRECTION · –ING"What are you looking at?" — the direction of your gaze

PHRASAL · –ING"I am looking for my keys." — look for = to search

Here look is a dynamic verb — the stative look = seem cannot do this.

Ache & Hurt — the pain exception

for pain in the body you can use Simple or Continuous — the meaning stays the same

✓ Both forms work

SIMPLE"My back aches." / "My feet hurt."
— usual, chronic pain; a fact

CONTINUOUS"My back is aching." / "My feet are hurting."
— the pain is happening right now

Native speakers understand both exactly the same way.

⚠ Hurt has extra meanings

DYNAMIC ONLY"Be careful, you are hurting me!"
— causing harm: an action, so only –ing

USUALLY SIMPLE"His words hurt me."
— emotional pain; passive: I was hurt

Here the forms are NOT interchangeable — the meaning differs.

Touch — stative or dynamic?

the meaning chooses the form: a state of contact vs the act of touching

✓ Match the form to the meaning

STATE · SIMPLE"The two houses touch at the roofline."
— a state of contact: also "It touches freezing point."

ACTION · –ING"He is touching the screen to see if it works."
— the physical act of touching

Both sentences are correct — the form follows the meaning.

⚠ The classic mistake

✗ WRONG"The houses are touching at the roofline."

KEEP IT SIMPLE"It touches freezing point." / "I was touched by your letter."
— levels & emotions never go continuous

Same rule as be, seem, belong — states live in simple tenses.
